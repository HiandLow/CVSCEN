\begin{abstract}
\vspace{-1em}
Estimating continuous treatment effects from observational data requires controlling for confounding, yet the relevant pre-treatment covariates are rarely known in advance.
Including all observed covariates to avoid omitting confounders can introduce variables predictive of treatment but not the outcome, as well as irrelevant variables, degrading finite-sample estimation accuracy.
Moreover, selecting only outcome-relevant covariates does not distinguish confounders from outcome predictors, as both are associated with the outcome but play different roles in causal estimation. 
We propose the \emph{Role-Aware Variable Selection and Effect Estimation Network} (RAVEN), an end-to-end framework that jointly selects outcome-relevant covariates, learns their causal roles, and estimates continuous treatment effects. 
RAVEN uses outcome-prediction feedback with sparsity regularization to select outcome-relevant covariates without prespecifying the selection size. 
Among these covariates, RAVEN distinguishes confounders from outcome predictors based on their relationship with treatment, favoring treatment-dependent covariates as confounders and treatment-independent covariates as outcome-predictors. 
We establish an estimation error decomposition for the average dose--response function, in which the effect of omitted covariates depends jointly on their treatment and outcome relevance,  providing theoretical support for causal role-aware selection. 
Experiments on synthetic and semi-synthetic benchmarks demonstrate improved dose--response estimation and accurate recovery of covariate roles.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Estimating causal effects from observational data is central to decision-making in medicine, econometrics, and the social sciences \citep{rosenbaum1983central,rubin2005causal}. 
Although randomized controlled trials provide a principled basis for causal estimation, practical, ethical, and financial constraints often necessitate reliance on observational data \citep{imbens2015causal}.
Under covariate adjustment, causal identification requires an appropriate set of pre-treatment covariates, but the variables needed to control confounding are rarely known a priori.
Practitioners therefore often adjust for all measured covariates to reduce the risk of omitting important confounders \citep{vander2011new}.


Many real-world interventions, including drug dosages and discount rates, vary continuously \citep{bica2020estimating, kazemi2024adversarially, nagalapatti2024continuous}.
Unlike binary settings, where causal estimation compares outcomes across a small number of treatment groups, continuous-treatment estimation requires learning how expected outcomes vary over an entire treatment domain  \citep{hirano2004propensity,imai2004causal}. 
This also makes covariate adjustment more challenging, as relationships between covariates and treatment, as well as covariate overlap, must be characterized across a continuum of treatment levels rather than a finite set of treatment groups.
Most continuous-treatment estimators, however, focus on flexibly modeling dose-response relationships while treating the observed covariates as a fixed input set, without explicitly distinguishing their roles in treatment assignment and outcome prediction \citep{nie2021vcnet, bica2020estimating}.



Treating all observed covariates equivalently can be problematic because they play distinct roles in treatment assignment and outcome generation.
Confounders are associated with both treatment and outcome and must be retained for valid adjustment, while outcome predictors are associated with the outcome but not treatment and can improve outcome modeling and estimation efficiency. 
In contrast, instrumental variables are associated with treatment but have no direct effect on the outcome, whereas irrelevant variables are associated with neither treatment nor outcome. 
Thus, once a sufficient adjustment set is available, including instrumental and irrelevant variables can increase finite-sample variance and introduce unnecessary nuisance dimensions without improving confounding control.
Instrumental variables can further amplify bias when residual unobserved confounding remains \citep{pearl2011invited,ding2017instrumental}. 
For continuous treatments, strong treatment--instrument relationships can additionally reduce effective overlap at some treatment levels, increasing estimation variance when observations are sparse. 
These challenges motivate causal role-aware variable selection that retains confounders and outcome predictors while excluding instrumental and irrelevant variables \citep{pearl2014confounding,ma2025simultaneous}.


Despite this need, existing variable-selection approaches do not fully address role-aware selection for continuous treatments. Many are designed for binary or discrete treatments, rely primarily on predictive relevance, provide only implicit filtering, or perform variable selection separately from treatment effect estimation \citep{greenewald2021high,vanderschueren2025autocate}.
VSCEN \citep{ma2025simultaneous}, for example, jointly optimizes variable selection and treatment effect estimation in an end-to-end manner, but is designed for binary treatments and requires the number of selected variables to be specified in advance.



Role-aware variable selection for continuous treatment effect estimation raises three key challenges. 
First, outcome relevance alone cannot distinguish confounders from outcome predictors, as both are associated with the outcome. 
Second, treatment dependence alone cannot distinguish confounders from instrumental variables, as both are associated with treatment. 
Role assignment must therefore combine outcome and treatment information without favoring variables merely because they strongly predict treatment.
Third, continuous treatment effect estimation requires modeling how covariates affect the outcome while flexibly capturing how the expected outcome changes with treatment level. 


To address these challenges, we propose the \textit{Role-Aware Variable Selection and Effect Estimation Network} (RAVEN), an end-to-end framework that jointly performs role-aware variable selection and continuous treatment effect estimation.
RAVEN addresses the first two challenges by combining outcome and treatment information to determine which covariates to retain and how they should be used. Outcome-prediction feedback identifies covariates that are useful for explaining the outcome, while sparsity regularization discourages unnecessary covariates and allows the number of selected variables to be learned from the data.
Among outcome-relevant covariates, treatment dependence then helps distinguish confounders from outcome predictors. Because strong treatment dependence can indicate either a confounder or an instrumental variable, RAVEN uses this information asymmetrically: weak treatment dependence discourages the confounder role, but strong treatment dependence alone does not favor it. 
RAVEN also encourages covariates assigned as outcome predictors to be independent of treatment. 
Together, these mechanisms allow RAVEN to select outcome-related covariates and distinguish confounders from outcome predictors without treating strong treatment association alone as evidence of confounding. 
To address the third challenge, RAVEN models the outcome using the selected covariates while allowing their contribution to vary with the treatment level.
Specifically, it combines a treatment-independent prediction based on the selected covariates with a treatment-conditioned component that captures how the outcome changes across treatment levels and covariate profiles. This structure enables flexible estimation of nonlinear dose--response relationships.



Our theoretical analysis further supports this role-aware design by decomposing the average dose--response function (ADRF) estimation error into errors due to residual confounding, outcome-model learning, and finite-sample averaging.
In particular, the residual confounding error depends jointly on how strongly omitted covariates are related to treatment and outcome, providing theoretical support for variable selection that considers both relationships rather than outcome relevance alone.


We evaluate RAVEN on a fully synthetic benchmark with controlled covariate roles and a semi-synthetic IHDP benchmark with real-world covariates. Across both settings, RAVEN achieves the lowest mean integrated squared error (MISE) and ADRF error among the evaluated methods while accurately identifying confounders and outcome predictors and largely excluding instrumental and irrelevant variables.

\section{Related Work}
\vspace{-0.5em}
\subsection{Continuous Treatment Effect Estimation}
Continuous treatment effect estimation is commonly studied through generalized propensity score and related potential-outcome frameworks \citep{hirano2004propensity, imai2004causal}. 
These approaches provide a principled foundation for identifying dose--response functions but often require accurate modeling of treatment assignment and can be sensitive to limited overlap in observational data.  
Recent deep learning methods use flexible neural architectures to capture complex nonlinear dose-response relationships. 
SCIGAN uses a generative adversarial framework to estimate counterfactual outcomes under continuous interventions \citep{bica2020estimating}. VCNet combines varying-coefficient neural networks with targeted regularization to estimate individualized dose--response curves \citep{nie2021vcnet}. 
DRNet \citep{schwab2020learning} partitions the continuous treatment space into intervals and separate prediction heads. 
Other approaches improve estimation through adversarially balanced representations \citep{kazemi2024adversarially}, gradient interpolation \citep{nagalapatti2024continuous}, or proximal causal learning \citep{wu2023doubly}. 
Despite these advances, existing methods typically treat the observed covariate set as fixed, without explicitly selecting covariates according to their roles in treatment assignment and outcome prediction. 


\subsection{Variable Selection for Causal Inference}
\vspace{-0.5em}
Variable selection is widely used in causal inference to improve estimation efficiency by removing irrelevant or harmful covariates, with classical regularization methods such as the Lasso selecting variables through coefficient shrinkage \citep{tibshirani1996regression}. 
However, predictive relevance alone cannot distinguish confounders from outcome predictors, as both can be associated with the outcome \citep{greenewald2021high}.
Robust causal estimators, such as Double/Debiased Machine Learning \citep{chernozhukov2018double} and Generalized Random Forests (GRF) \citep{athey2019generalized}, use flexible nuisance models for high-dimensional treatment effect estimation, with recent extensions to continuous treatments \citep{zenati2025double}, but do not explicitly select and assign roles to individual covariates.
Other approaches reduce nuisance information through automated feature and model selection, as in AutoCATE \citep{vanderschueren2025autocate}, or representation learning, as in the information-bottleneck-based IBEX \citep{van2026continuous}. 
However, neither approach explicitly distinguishes individual covariates by their relationships with treatment and outcome.
VSCEN \citep{ma2025simultaneous} jointly selects confounders and outcome predictors while estimating binary treatment effects, but requires prespecified selection size for each role. In contrast, 
RAVEN jointly learns whether each covariate is excluded or assigned to the confounder or outcome-predictor role while estimating continuous treatment effects, without prespecifying the selection size.

\section{Background}
\vspace{-0.5em}
\subsection{Continuous Treatment Effect Estimation}
We consider continuous treatment effect estimation under the potential outcome framework \citep{rubin2005causal, imbens2015causal}. 
For unit $i$, let $A_i \in \mathcal{A}$ denote the observed continuous treatment and $Y_i^a$ the potential outcome under treatment level $a \in  \mathcal{A}$.
Unlike binary settings, continuous treatment effect estimation characterizes how expected potential outcomes vary over a continuum of treatment levels \citep{hirano2004propensity, imai2004causal}.
The average dose--response function (ADRF) and conditional average dose--response function (CADRF) are defined as $\mu(a):=\mathbb{E}[Y^a]$, and $\mu(a,\mathbf{x}):=\mathbb{E}[Y^a\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}]$, respectively. The CADRF characterizes the expected outcome at treatment level $a$ for units with covariates  \(\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}\), while the ADRF represents the corresponding population-level expected outcome and satisfies $\mu(a) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}[\mu(a,\mathbf{X})]$.

\subsection{Identification from Observational Data}
Identification from observational data relies on the standard assumptions of consistency, weak unconfoundedness and positivity 
\citep{rosenbaum1983central, hirano2004propensity, imai2004causal}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Consistency.} if $A_i=a$, then  $Y_i=Y_i^a$.
    \item \textbf{Weak unconfoundedness.} $Y_i^a \perp\!\!\!\perp A_i \mid \mathbf{X}_i$.
    \item \textbf{Positivity.}  
    $f_{A\mid \mathbf{X}}(a\mid \mathbf{x})>0$ for all $a\in\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathbf{x}$ in the support of $\mathbf{X}$.
\end{itemize}
We further partition the observed covariates as $\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{X}_C \cup \mathbf{X}_P \cup \mathbf{X}_I \cup \mathbf{X}_N$, 
where $\mathbf{X}_C$, $\mathbf{X}_P$, $\mathbf{X}_I$, and $\mathbf{X}_N$ denote confounders, outcome predictors, instrumental variables, and irrelevant variables, respectively. 
When $\textbf{S}= \mathbf{X}_C \cup \mathbf{X}_P$ is \(c\)-equivalent to  \(\mathbf{X}\), adjustment for $\textbf{S}$ provides the same confounding control as adjustment for  $\mathbf{X}$ \citep{pearl2014confounding, greenewald2021high}, allowing instrumental and irrelevant variables to be excluded without compromisng confounding adjustment. 


\vspace{-0.5em}
\subsection{Differential Variable Selection}
\vspace{-0.5em}
Variable selection is inherently discrete and non-differentiable, making joint optimization with neural networks difficult. The Concrete or Gumbel--Softmax relaxation provides a differential approximation to categorical selection \citep{maddison2017concrete,jang2017categorical}.
For categorical probabilities \(\boldsymbol{\pi}=(\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_K)\), 
a relaxed sample \(\boldsymbol{y}=(y_1,\ldots,y_K)\) is given by
\begin{equation}
    y_k =
    \frac{
        \exp\left((\log \pi_k + g_k)/\tau\right)
    }{
        \sum_{m=1}^{K}
        \exp\left((\log \pi_m + g_m)/\tau\right)
    },
    k=1,\ldots,K,
\end{equation}
where $g_K$ are independent standard Gumbel variables and \(\tau>0\) is the temperature. As $\tau$ decreases, $\boldsymbol{y}$ approches a one-hot categorical sample; for $\tau>0$, it remains differentiable and can serve as a soft selection mask optimized jointly with downstream neural-network parameters. 
}

\section{RAVEN}
\label{sec:RAVEN}
\vspace{-1em}
RAVEN is an end-to-end neural network for causal role-aware variable selection and continuous treatment effect estimation.
Given observations $\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}_i,a_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ of pre-treatment covariates, continuous treatment levels, and outcomes, it couples a differentiable covariate selector with a dual-stream outcome network (\cref{fig:raven_overview}).

The selector learns which covariates to select and their roles, producing separate masked inputs for confounders and outcome predictors (\cref{sec:role_aware_variable_selection}).
The outcome network separately encodes these inputs and feeds their concatenated representation to baseline and response streams.
Only the response stream receives treatment information through feature-wise affine modulation, and their outputs are summed to predict the outcome (\cref{sec:outcome_prediction}).
The selector and outcome network are trained jointly (\cref{sec:end_to_end_training}).
After training, evaluating the network at different treatment levels yields dose--response estimates, which are averaged over covariate profiles to estimate the ADRF.
\vspace{-0.5em}
\subsection{Role-Aware Variable Selection}
\label{sec:role_aware_variable_selection}
RAVEN uses a differentiable selector to assign each pre-treatment covariate to one of three roles: excluded, confounder, or outcome predictor.
These assignments determine which covariates enter the outcome network and how they are grouped into confounder and outcome-predictor inputs. 
The selector is learned jointly with the outcome network using predictive and treatment-related signals (\cref{sec:end_to_end_training}).

\textbf{Role-Assignment Logits.}
For each pre-treatment covariate $X_j$, the selector maintains a learnable logit vector $\boldsymbol{\ell}_j=(\ell_{j,0},\ell_{j,C},\ell_{j,P})\in\mathbb R^3$ over $\mathcal R=\{0,C,P\}$, corresponding to exclusion, confounder, and outcome-predictor roles.
The excluded role covers covariates not retained for adjustment or outcome modeling, which may include instrumental and irrelevant variables.
Each covariate's logit vector is shared across all observations. The confounder and outcome-predictor logits are initialized to the same value  $\ell_{\mathrm{init}}$ and the exclusion logit to zero, so that no role is favored at the start of training; $\ell_{\mathrm{init}}$ only sets the initial inclination to retain covariates.

\textbf{Treatment-Dependence Guidance.}
Because confounders affect treatment assignment whereas outcome predictors do not, whether a covariate depends on the treatment is a natural criterion for distinguishing the two roles. For the training-sample vectors $\mathbf x_{:j}=(x_{1,j},\ldots,x_{n,j})^\top$ and $\mathbf a=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)^\top$, define $\rho_j=\widehat{\operatorname{HSIC}}(\mathbf x_{:j},\mathbf a)$, where HSIC denotes the Hilbert--Schmidt Independence Criterion \citep{gretton2005measuring,gretton2007kernel}, computed with Gaussian kernels whose bandwidth is the median of the nonzero pairwise distances so that binary covariates are handled as well. Since the magnitude of $\rho_j$ depends on the sample size and on the covariate's distribution, we calibrate it against chance: with $\rho_j^{(1)},\ldots,\rho_j^{(B)}$ the statistics obtained after $B$ random permutations of $\mathbf a$, and $\bar\rho_j^{\,\pi}$ and $s_j^{\pi}$ their mean and standard deviation, let $t_j=(\rho_j-\bar\rho_j^{\,\pi})/s_j^{\pi}$ and $q_j=\Phi_{\mathcal N}\bigl(\Phi_{\mathcal N}^{-1}(0.95)-t_j\bigr)\in(0,1)$, where $\Phi_{\mathcal N}$ is the standard normal distribution function; $q_j$ is close to one when there is no evidence of treatment dependence and close to zero when the evidence is clear. We use $q_j$ to define role-specific costs
\begin{equation}
\label{Eq7}
c_{j,C}=1+\omega\,q_j,\qquad c_{j,P}=1+\omega\,(1-q_j),
\end{equation}
which make the confounder role costly for covariates without evidence of treatment dependence and the outcome-predictor role costly for covariates with it; the costs enter the training objective through the sparsity term (\cref{sec:end_to_end_training}). Since every retained covariate pays at least the base cost, the guidance never makes retention cheaper, so strong treatment dependence alone cannot promote an instrumental variable into the adjustment set.

\textbf{Differentiable Role Assignment.}
At epoch $e$, we anneal the Gumbel--Softmax temperature as $\tau^{(e)}=\max\{\tau_{\min},\tau_{\mathrm{init}}\exp(-\kappa e)\}$, with $\kappa>0$, to gradually concentrate the relaxed assignments. Using the logits $\boldsymbol{\ell}_j$  and independent standard Gumbel variables $g_{j,r}^{(e)}$, we obtain a differentiable stochastic assignment through the Gumbel--Softmax relaxation \citep{jang2017categorical,maddison2017concrete}:
\[
z_{j,r}^{(e)}
=
\frac{\exp[({\ell}_{j,r}+g_{j,r}^{(e)})/\tau^{(e)}]}
{\sum_{r'\in\mathcal R}\exp[({\ell}_{j,r'}+g_{j,r'}^{(e)})/\tau^{(e)}]},
\qquad r\in\mathcal R.
\]
The vector $\boldsymbol{z}_j^{(e)}=(z_{j,0}^{(e)},z_{j,C}^{(e)},z_{j,P}^{(e)})$ lies on the probability simplex and approximates a discrete role assignment.
For $r\in\{C,P\}$, define the mask $\mathbf m^{r,(e)}=(z_{1,r}^{(e)},\ldots,z_{d,r}^{(e)})$ and masked input $\mathbf x_i^r=\mathbf m^{r,(e)}\odot\mathbf x_i$, where $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication.

\textbf{Outcome-Relevance Feedback.}
The masks determine which covariate information reaches the outcome network (\cref{sec:outcome_prediction}).
During joint training, gradients from the factual outcome loss defined in \cref{sec:end_to_end_training} propagate through the masks to the role-assignment logits, encouraging covariates useful for outcome prediction to receive greater assignment probability in the $C$ or $P$ role.
Together with sparsity regularization, this feedback discourages unnecessary inclusion without fixing the number of selected covariates.
%The one-sided guidance complements this feedback and the treatment-independence penalty on the $P$ inputs, rather than determining causal roles on its own.
\vspace{-0.5em}
\subsection{Dual-Stream Outcome Model}
\label{sec:outcome_prediction}
RAVEN models outcomes from the selected covariates while allowing their contribution to outcome prediction to vary with the treatment level.
The outcome network maps the treatment level $a$ and masked covariates $\mathbf{s}_i=(\mathbf{x}_i^C,\mathbf{x}_i^P)$ to a prediction $F_\Theta(a,\mathbf{s}_i)$.
It combines a covariate-only baseline stream with a treatment-conditioned response stream.
%The neural encoders, stream-specific adapters, and prediction heads are implemented with residual multilayer perceptron blocks.

\textbf{Role-Specific Encoding and Dual Streams.}
The masked inputs are separately encoded as $h_i^C=\Phi_C(\mathbf{x}_i^C)$ and $h_i^P=\Phi_P(\mathbf{x}_i^P)$.
Their concatenated representation $h_i=[h_i^C;h_i^P]$ is passed to two nonlinear adapters: $h_{i,\mathrm{base}}=q_{\mathrm{base}}(h_i)$ and $h_{i,\mathrm{resp}}=q_{\mathrm{resp}}(h_i)$.
Both streams therefore receive information from both selected roles.
The baseline stream produces $f_{\mathrm{base}}(h_{i,\mathrm{base}})$ without directly receiving the treatment value.
The response stream conditions its covariate representation on $a$ through the affine modulation defined below.

\textbf{Treatment-Conditioned Affine Modulation.}
To help model nonlinear treatment responses, we encode the normalized treatment $a\in[0,1]$ using sinusoidal features at $L$ different frequencies \citep{mildenhall2020nerf,tancik2020fourier}: $\phi(a)=[a,\{\sin(2^\ell\pi a),\cos(2^\ell\pi a)\}_{\ell=0}^{L-1}]^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{1+2L}$.
Neural maps generate feature-wise scale and shift parameters, $\gamma(a)=g_\gamma(\phi(a))$ and $\delta(a)=g_\delta(\phi(a))$.
Following feature-wise linear modulation \citep{perez2018film}, we define $\operatorname{Mod}(h,a)=h\odot(1+\gamma(a))+\delta(a)$.
This mechanism injects treatment information directly into the response representation rather than relying solely on concatenation of a scalar treatment with the covariates.
The residual scale $1+\gamma(a)$ allows an identity transformation when the modulation parameters are zero.

The two stream outputs are summed to obtain the combined prediction:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:raven_outcome}
F_\Theta(a,\mathbf{s}_i)
=
f_{\mathrm{base}}(h_{i,\mathrm{base}})
+
f_{\mathrm{resp}}\bigl(\operatorname{Mod}(h_{i,\mathrm{resp}},a)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Through treatment-conditioned modulation, the response stream can model interactions between treatment and the selected covariates.
During training, we evaluate Eq.~(\ref{eq:raven_outcome}) at the observed treatment level $a_i$ to obtain the factual prediction $\widehat{y}_i=F_\Theta(a_i,\mathbf{s}_i)$.

\subsection{Joint Training and Effect Estimation}
\label{sec:end_to_end_training}
RAVEN jointly learns covariate selection, role assignment, and outcome prediction by combining factual outcome loss with a sparsity term whose role-specific costs implement the treatment-dependence guidance.
We optimize the role-assignment logits and outcome-network parameters using

% \begin{equation}
% \label{eq:raven_training}
% \mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_Y+\lambda_{\mathrm{hsic}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hsic}}+w^{(e)}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}},
% \end{equation}
% where $\lambda_{\mathrm{hsic}}\geq0$ controls treatment-independence regularization and $w^{(e)}$ activates sparsity after initial exploration.
% The one-sided guidance additionally enters through the adjusted logits in \cref{Eq7}.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:raven_training}
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_Y+\lambda_{\mathrm{reg}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\bigl(c_{j,C}\,z_{j,C}^{(e)}+c_{j,P}\,z_{j,P}^{(e)}\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $c_{j,C}$ and $c_{j,P}$ are the role-specific costs in \cref{Eq7} and $\lambda_{\mathrm{reg}}>0$ is the cost of retaining a covariate.
The factual outcome loss provides predictive feedback to both the selector and outcome network.
We use the Huber loss \citep{huber1992robust}, $\mathcal{L}_Y=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{\delta_H}(y_i-\widehat{y}_i)$, where $\ell_{\delta_H}(u)=u^2/2$ if $|u|\leq\delta_H$ and $\ell_{\delta_H}(u)=\delta_H(|u|-\delta_H/2)$ otherwise, with $\delta_H=1.0$.

% To discourage treatment-related information in the outcome-predictor inputs, we impose $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hsic}}=\widehat{\operatorname{HSIC}}(\{\mathbf{x}_i^P\}_{i=1}^{n},\{a_i\}_{i=1}^{n})$, complementing the one-sided guidance for role separation.

% To avoid removing covariates before their predictive utility is learned, we delay sparsity regularization until $\tau^{(e)}\leq\tau_s$.
% Specifically, we set $w^{(e)}=\mathbf{1}\{\tau^{(e)}\leq\tau_s\}$, with $\tau_s=1.0$ by default.
% The penalty $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{reg},C}\sum_{j=1}^{d}z_{j,C}^{(e)}+\lambda_{\mathrm{reg},P}\sum_{j=1}^{d}z_{j,P}^{(e)}$ independently controls relaxed inclusion in the two selected roles.
% This encourages compact selection without imposing a predefined selection cardinality.

The sparsity term charges every retained covariate at least $\lambda_{\mathrm{reg}}$, summed over covariates so that $\lambda_{\mathrm{reg}}$ is the price of retaining one covariate regardless of the dimension $d$. Retention is therefore governed by the trade-off between this price and the covariate's contribution to the factual outcome loss, while the role-specific costs determine whether a retained covariate is assigned the confounder or the outcome-predictor role: the outcome loss does not distinguish the two roles, so the cheaper role, which depends only on $q_j$, is selected. 

\textbf{Dose--Response Estimation.}
After training, each covariate is assigned to $\widehat{r}_j=\arg\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\ell_{j,r}$, yielding hard masks $\widehat{m}_{C,j}=\mathbf{1}\{\widehat{r}_j=C\}$ and $\widehat{m}_{P,j}=\mathbf{1}\{\widehat{r}_j=P\}$.
For unit $i$, the resulting input is $\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_i=(\widehat{\mathbf{m}}_C\odot\mathbf{x}_i,\widehat{\mathbf{m}}_P\odot\mathbf{x}_i)$.
Varying the treatment level while holding this input fixed yields $\widehat{\mu}_\Theta(a,\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_i)=F_\Theta(a,\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_i)$.
The ADRF is estimated as $\widehat{\mu}(a)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\mu}_\Theta(a,\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_i)$.
The subsequent theoretical analysis characterizes its error through residual confounding from variable selection, outcome-model learning error, and empirical averaging error.

\section{Theoretical Analysis}
We provide a finite-sample error bound for the ADRF estimator induced by RAVEN.
Our bound decomposes the ADRF error into three terms.
First, let $\Delta_A(\hat S)$ and $\Delta_Y(\hat S)$ bound the residual treatment-assignment and outcome sensitivities of omitted covariates after selecting $\hat S$.
With selected-covariate positivity constant $c_A>0$, define $\mathcal E_{\rm sel}:=\frac{1}{c_A}\Delta_A(\hat S)\Delta_Y(\hat S)$.
This term captures residual confounding due to variable selection.

Second, because RAVEN is trained with the Huber factual loss, let $\mathcal L_n^H$ be the corresponding Huber-loss class induced by the outcome model and selection maps, uniformly bounded by $B_H$. 
Here, $\mathfrak R_n(\cdot)$ denotes empirical Rademacher complexity, which controls the uniform concentration of a function class. 
Define
\[
    \gamma_H(n,\delta):=2\mathfrak R_n(\mathcal L_n^H)+B_H\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2n}}.
\]
Let $\mathcal B_{\rm app}$ and $\mathcal B_{\rm opt}$ denote approximation and optimization error bounds measured in Huber risk.
For example, under smoothness of the selected-covariate outcome regression, $\mathcal B_{\rm app}$ can be taken as a standard neural approximation bound, while $\mathcal B_{\rm opt}$ can be set to the empirical optimization tolerance.
Under Huber-risk consistency, local curvature, and a treatment-domain transfer condition, the prediction error is bounded by
\[
    \mathcal E_{\rm pred}:=C_{\rm tr}\sqrt{\mathcal B_{\rm app}+2\gamma_H(n,\delta)+\mathcal B_{\rm opt}}.
\]

Third, empirical averaging introduces an additional error because the population covariate distribution is replaced by the empirical distribution. 
Let $\Gamma_{\rm avg}(n,\delta)$ be a high-probability uniform concentration bound satisfying
\[
    \sup_{a\in\mathcal A}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat\mu_\Theta(a,\hat s_i)-\mathbb{E}[\hat\mu_\Theta(a,\hat S)]\right|\leq\Gamma_{\rm avg}(n,\delta)
\]
with probability at least $1-\delta$. 
We define $\mathcal E_{\rm avg}:=\Gamma_{\rm avg}(n,\delta)$.
Finally, set $\varepsilon_{\rm cv} := \mathcal E_{\rm sel} + \mathcal E_{\rm pred} + \mathcal E_{\rm avg}$.

\begin{theorem}[Finite-Sample ADRF Error Bound]
\label{thm:finite_adrf_bound} 
Under selected-covariate positivity, residual sensitivity control, and the prediction and averaging bounds defined above, with probability at least $1-\delta$,
\[    
    \sup_{a\in\mathcal A} \left| \hat{\mu}(a)-\mu(a) \right| \leq \varepsilon_{\rm cv}. 
\] 
\end{theorem} 
Theorem~\ref{thm:finite_adrf_bound} decomposes the ADRF estimation error into residual confounding from variable selection, factual outcome-model error controlled through the Huber risk, and empirical averaging error.
The selection term depends on the product $\Delta_A(\hat S)\Delta_Y(\hat S)$, reflecting that omitted covariates induce bias primarily when they are both treatment-associated and outcome-relevant.
Thus, the result does not require exact recovery of every causal role; it requires the selected covariates to leave small residual confounding.
The proof and concrete concentration bounds for $\gamma_H(n,\delta)$ and $\Gamma_{\rm avg}(n,\delta)$ are provided in Appendix~\ref{Proofs for the Theorems}.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Overview}
We organize the experiments around three research questions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt, parsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (Treatment Effect Estimation).} How accurately does RAVEN estimate conditional and population-level dose-response functions?

    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Variable Selection).} Does RAVEN retain covariates relevant to confounding adjustment or outcome modeling while excluding instrumental and irrelevant variables? 

    \item \textbf{RQ3 (Mechanism Analysis).} How do dependence-based guidance and HSIC regularization contribute to role-aware variable selection and dose-response estimation? 
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Experimental Setting}
We evaluate RAVEN on two benchmarks:
(i) a fully synthetic dataset with controlled covariate roles and known dose-response functions (\cref{Exp: fully synthetic}), and 
(ii) a semi-synthetic dataset that combines real-world covariates with synthetically generated treatments and outcomes (\cref{Exp: semi-synthetic}).
Both settings provide ground-truth dose-response functions and causal roles, enabling quantitative evaluation of treatment effect estimation and variable selection, which cannot be directly assessed using fully observational data. 

\textbf{Baselines.}
We compare RAVEN with representative continuous treatment effect estimators, including VCNet \citep{nie2021vcnet}, DRNet \citep{schwab2020learning}, SCIGAN \citep{bica2020estimating}, GIKS \citep{nagalapatti2024continuous}, IBEX \citep{van2026continuous}, DDMLCT \citep{colangelo2025double}, and ACFR \citep{kazemi2024adversarially}.

\textbf{Implementation Details.}
RAVEN parameterizes role assignments using a \(d\times3\) logit matrix and anneals the Gumbel--Softmax temperature from \(10.0\) to \(0.1\).
The dual-stream outcome model uses residual MLP blocks with LayerNorm and SiLU activations, NeRF-style treatment encoding, and treatment-conditioned affine modulation.
Using a train--validation--test split, we tune all methods on the validation set by factual outcome loss via Optuna and report test-set performance.
%Unlike general deep learning, hyperparameter tuning in causal inference tasks is uniquely challenging due to the absence of ground-truth counterfactuals. 
%However, since our benchmarks provide the true dose-response functions, we directly use MISE as the tuning metric for all methods.
% Because the synthetic and semi-synthetic benchmarks provide ground-truth dose--response functions, we select hyperparameters for all methods using validation MISE, ensuring a common tuning criterion across methods.

\textbf{Evaluation Metrics.}
Let \(\widehat{\mu}_i(a)\) denote the dose--response estimate produced by a given method for evaluation unit \(i\); for RAVEN,
\(\widehat{\mu}_i(a)=\widehat{\mu}_{\Theta}(a,\widehat{\mathbf{s}}_i)\).
Using an equally spaced treatment grid \(\mathcal{G}\) over the 5th--95th percentiles of the observed treatment distribution, we report
\vspace{-1.5em}
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{MISE}
&=
\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{ev}}|\mathcal{G}|}
\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{ev}}}
\sum_{a\in\mathcal{G}}
\left[
\widehat{\mu}_i(a)-\mu(a,\mathbf{x}_i)
\right]^2,
\\
\epsilon_{\mathrm{ADRF}}
&=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}
\sum_{a\in\mathcal{G}}
\left|
\widehat{\mu}(a)-\mu(a)
\right|,
\end{aligned}
\]
where
\(\widehat{\mu}(a)=
N_{\mathrm{ev}}^{-1}
\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{ev}}}
\widehat{\mu}_i(a)\).
For each role \(r\in\{C,P,C\cup P\}\), we additionally report the false discovery rate (FDR) and true positive rate (TPR).

\textbf{Evaluation Range.} 
To avoid extrapolation to sparsely supported treatment regions, where limited overlap can yield unstable estimates \citep{petersen2012diagnosing, kennedy2017non}, we compute MISE and ADRF error over the range between the 5th and 95th percentiles of the observed treatment distribution, following standard approaches to limited overlap \cite{crump2009dealing}.

For each replicate, we generate \(N=2000\) observations with \(\mathbf{x}_i\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I_{100})\).
We assign \(X_{1:5}\) to the confounder role, \(X_{6:10}\) to the outcome-predictor role, \(X_{11:15}\) to the instrumental-variable role, and \(X_{16:100}\) to the irrelevant role. 
Let \(\mathbf{c}^{t},\mathbf{c}^{o}\in\mathbb{R}^{100}\) denote the treatment and outcome coefficient vector.

and the observed outcome is generated as $y_i=\mu(a_i,\mathbf{x}_i)+\epsilon_i^{Y}, \quad \epsilon_i^{Y}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.1^2)$.
The first term captures covariate-dependent baseline outcomes, while the second specifies a nonlinear saturating treatment-response component.

\subsection{Evaluation on Semi-Synthetic IHDP Benchmark}
\label{Exp: semi-synthetic}

\textbf{Data Generation.}
We use the 25 IHDP covariates to generate a continuous treatment and outcome as follows. Covariates are assigned roles by their meaning: confounders $c=\{1,2,3,5,6,9,10\}$ (infant health and maternal socioeconomic status), outcome predictors $p=\{4,7,8,13,15\}$ (child and pregnancy factors), instruments $w=\{19,\ldots,25\}$ (study sites), and irrelevant covariates $\{11,12,14,16,17,18\}$. Inside the generating process, each covariate is standardized with full-data statistics, $z_j$, so that every coefficient contributes comparable variance regardless of prevalence.

The treatment is $a=\sigma(\ell_a)$, and treatment and outcome are generated as
\begin{align}
\ell_a &= h(\mathbf{z}_c) + \boldsymbol{\delta}^{\top}\mathbf{z}_w + \eta, \\
Y &= \mu(\mathbf{z}_c,\mathbf{z}_p) + \frac{\sin(3\pi a)}{1.2-a} + g(\mathbf{z})\, f\big(a; a^*(\mathbf{z}_c)\big) + \epsilon,
\end{align}
with $\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.6^2)$ and $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.5^2)$.
Here $h$ and $\mu$ are sums of per-covariate terms (linear, saturating, or V-shaped), so every confounder and outcome predictor has its own effect;
$f(a;a^*)=(r e^{1-r})^3$ with $r=a/a^*$ is an inverted-U dose curve peaking at a subject-specific $a^*\in[0.3,0.6]$;
and $g$ is a benefit size that varies across children and can be negative. The covariate-free term $\sin(3\pi a)/(1.2-a)$ gives an oscillating population dose response

All coefficients are listed in Table~\ref{tab:ihdp_dgp} in the appendix. 
This benchmark combines nonlinear, confounded treatment assignment, heterogeneous treatment effects, instruments, irrelevant covariates, and naturally correlated real-world covariates.

\section{Conclusion}
We introduced RAVEN, an end-to-end framework for continuous treatment effect estimation with explicit causal role-aware variable selection. 
RAVEN jointly learns confounder, outcome-predictor, and excluded roles with a dual-stream outcome model, combining one-sided dependence guidance, HSIC-based regularization, and role-specific sparsity. 
Experiments on synthetic and semi-synthetic benchmarks show improved dose-response estimation while recovering covariates relevant to adjustment and outcome modeling.

RAVEN assumes an observed sufficient adjustment set, does not address unobserved confounding or severe overlap violations, and is evaluated only where causal roles are known. Future work will study real-world applications, more complex treatment settings, and theoretical guarantees for role recovery and dose-response estimation.

\subsection{Datasets.}
\textbf{Synthetic Dataset.}
\textbf{IHDP Dataset.} The Infant Health and Development Program (IHDP) dataset is widely utilized as a standard benchmark in causal inference to evaluate treatment effect estimation algorithms. Originally collected from a randomized study targeting low-birth-weight and premature infants, the dataset provides a rich set of real-world covariates. 

Specifically, we use 25 covariates for each subject, encompassing both child characteristics (e.g., birth weight, head circumference) and maternal attributes (e.g., maternal age, education level, and behaviors during pregnancy). Among the 25 covariates, 6 are continuous, and 19 are binary.  As model inputs, the continuous covariates are standardized to zero mean and unit variance, and the binary covariates are coded as $\{0, 1\}$. 

While the IHDP dataset is traditionally used for binary treatment settings, we adapt it for our continuous treatment semi-synthetic experiment. We detach the original treatment and outcome variables, retaining only the 25-dimensional real-world covariate matrix  $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 25}$. These covariates exhibit inherent, natural correlation structures and complex multicollinearity. By systematically partitioning these real-world covariates into distinct causal roles—confounders, outcome predictors, and instrumental variables—we obtain a realistic benchmark for jointly estimating continuous treatment effects and selecting causal variables.

\subsection{Baselines.}
We compare the proposed method with representative baselines.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Base (Mean Baseline): A naive baseline that estimates the average outcome of the population.
    \item VCNet (Variable Coefficient Network) \citep{nie2021vcnet}: A model for continuous treatments that uses varying coefficient layers to approximate the dose-response function. 
    \item DRNet (Dose Response Network) \citep{schwab2020learning}: A deep learning architecture designed for continuous treatments that partitions the dosage range into multiple strata and learns separate head networks to estimate the individual dose-response function.
    \item SCIGAN \citep{bica2020estimating}: A generative framework that utilizes Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) to predict counterfactual outcomes for continuous-valued interventions. 
    \item GIKS (Gradient Interpolation and Kernel Smoothing) \citep{nagalapatti2024continuous}: A recent method that leverages gradient interpolation and kernel smoothing to robustly estimate continuous treatment effects.
    \item IBEX (Cauchy-Schwarz Bottleneck) \citep{van2026continuous}: A representation learning approach that uses an Information Bottleneck based on Cauchy-Schwarz divergence to compress non-causal variables.
    \item DDMLCT (Double Machine Learning for Continuous Treatments) \citep{colangelo2025double}: A double machine learning framework designed for continuous treatments with high-dimensional nuisance parameters.
    \item ACFR (Adversarial Counterfactual Regression) \citep{kazemi2024adversarially}: A deep learning method that utilizes adversarially balanced representations to mitigate confounding bias in continuous treatment effect estimation.
    \item RAVEN (Ours): Our proposed end-to-end framework that simultaneously performs causal role-aware variable selection and continuous treatment effect estimation. 
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Implementation Details}

\textbf{Network Architecture.} The RAVEN framework is implemented as an end-to-end neural network consisting of two primary functional modules.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Variable Selection Layer}  This layer maintains a learnable logit matrix of size $d \times 3$, where $d$ is the number of input covariates. During training, it produces continuous masks $M \in [0, 1]^d$ using the Gumbel-Softmax trick to ensure stable gradient flow, while switching to discrete one-hot selections $M \in \{0, 1\}^d$ during test via argmax. The Gumbel-Softmax temperature $\tau$ anneals from 10.0 to 0.1 over the training epochs. HSIC regularization is further applied to penalize dependencies between selected variables and the treatment, where applicable.
    
    \item \textbf{Outcome Prediction Layer} receives the covariates masked as confounders ($x_c$) and outcome predictors ($x_p$), alongside the 1-dimensional observed continuous treatment. Its internal architecture utilizes a dual-stream feature extraction strategy: both $x_c$ and $x_p$ are independently processed by deep feature extractors containing Residual Blocks and LayerNorm, utilizing SiLU activations. The continuous treatment is expanded into a higher-dimensional space using NeRF-style positional encoding (e.g., $a, \sin(2^k \pi a), \cos(2^k \pi a)$). The extracted confounder representations are then dynamically modulated via a treatment-conditioned affine transformation (generating scale $\gamma$ and shift $\beta$). The prognostic representations are kept strictly independent of the treatment. Finally, the modulated representations are aggregated and passed through a prediction MLP to output the scalar response.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Training and Hyperparameter Tuning Details.} 
All experiments were implemented using the PyTorch framework. To ensure a fair comparison and optimal performance, we systematically tuned the hyperparameters---including the learning rate and weight decay---for both our proposed RAVEN model and all baseline methods. For RAVEN, the network parameters were optimized using the Adam optimizer. To ensure stable convergence throughout training, we coupled Adam with a StepLR scheduler that decays the learning rate by a factor of $\gamma=0.97$ every 100 epochs, and applied $L_2$ regularization (weight decay) to prevent overfitting and control the complexity of the deep neural network.


% For robust evaluation, the datasets were randomly partitioned into training, validation, and test sets using a $63{:}27{:}10$ split ratio. The training set was used to update the model weights, while the validation set was utilized exclusively for hyperparameter optimization via Optuna-based Bayesian optimization using the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) algorithm to efficiently explore the search space. Because our benchmarks provide ground-truth dose-response functions, we directly minimized the out-of-sample Mean Integrated Squared Error (MISE) on the validation set during tuning. 
For robust evaluation, the datasets were randomly partitioned into training, validation, and test sets with a $63{:}27{:}10$ split ratio, using the same fixed partition for every method. The training set was used to update the model weights, the validation set exclusively for hyperparameter selection, and the test set only for the final evaluation; no ground-truth information was used for tuning. Hyperparameters were optimized with optuna using the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) sampler with a fixed seed. For every method, the tuning objective was the factial outcome prediction loss on the validation set, which is observable in practive.Tuning proceeded in two stages: 20 trials on a single replicate, whose three best configuration were re-evaluated on three further replicates, followed by 20 trials in which each configuration was scored by its validation loss averaged over three replicates. Each training rus used a fixed random seed, so all trials are reproducible. For RAVEN, the sparsity weight $\lambda_{\mathrm{reg}}$ was then selected by one-standard error rule\citep{breiman1984classification}: holding the other tuned hyperparameters fixed, we evaluated each candidate $\lambda_{\mathrm{reg}}$ on the same three replicates and chose the largest value whose mean validation loss was within one standard error of the minimum. This facors the sparser of statiscally indistinguishable adjustment sets; the baselines have no selection penalty and use the configurtion with the minimum validation loss.

The search spaces were carefully selected to cover standard configurations and authors' recommendations for continuous treatment effect estimation. The comprehensive search ranges for RAVEN and the baseline models are summarized in Table \ref{tab:hyper_appendix}. The final performance of the causal effect estimation was evaluated exclusively on the held-out test set.
